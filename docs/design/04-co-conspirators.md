# Co-conspirators

"The driving force of the game." Each co-conspirator is a **card**: they
raise some of your stats, unlock new areas and abilities, open up events and
interactions, and strengthen the plot. *(OneNote › 3. Co-Conspirators)*

> **Decided ([D4](../decisions.md)):** the deck is built by drafting
> co-conspirators. The [paper prototype](../prototype/cards.md#co-conspirators-8)
> has a first 8.

## Recruiting them: the draft

- You **draft** co-conspirators from a selection — say **3 to start**.
- The pool **persists between rounds** and changes as you go: new people
  turn up when your stats improve, when Pressure crosses a threshold, or
  after a decision in an event. Others **lose interest** and leave.
- **Availability depends on your stats.** A military man needs a Military
  *floor*; a better one needs a higher floor — and if you climb past it,
  they may no longer be on offer.
- **Pressure shapes the pool.** More Pressure brings better candidates, up
  to a point; too much brings unstable, unreliable ones.
- **The crew grows.** Eventually you're forced to draft a *wide* pool and
  keep all of them happy. *(OneNote › 1. Variables)*
- **Honorifics change who comes knocking.** Known as a cheater? Some
  candidates will want you to lose a gambling match to them, or pay heavily
  to prove yourself — and more people with a similar trait will seek you
  out. *(OneNote › Honorifics)*

*(OneNote › 3. Co-Conspirators unless noted)*

## What they cost

| Way in | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Requirement** | Meet a minimum — e.g. high enough Charm and they join willingly. |
| **Spend a stat** | Pay with a stat. A lout or a womaniser might cost you **Influence** to take on. Some cost Charm. |
| **Money** | Some simply cost money ("hiring" them). |
| **Charm** | Win them over. *(Basics)* |
| **Blackmail** | Spend money **and Pressure** to force them on side — at the cost of their happiness, more suspicion, and still more Pressure. |
| **Uninvited** | Some join **without your consent**, especially as Pressure rises — and may blackmail *you*. *(3. Co-Conspirators; blackmail: 1.1 Pressure)* |

*(OneNote › 3. Co-Conspirators unless noted)*

## What they do for you

- **Stats.** Their standing adds onto yours — see
  [Stats and checks](03-stats-and-checks.md). *(3.1)*
- **Cards.** Each brings their own cards into your pools — good or bad
  (think Slay the Spire curses). You **can't** add or remove their cards
  one by one; you add or remove the *person*. *(3.2)*
- **One-time cards**, usable outside events — e.g. a propaganda campaign:
  costs money, raises Pressure, boosts one of your stats. *(3.2)*
- **Access.** Each gives you reach into certain areas, people and means — the
  pieces you need for a particular plan. *(3.3)*

### Example: two ways to the same Emperor *(3.3)*

You've learned he enjoys the company of actors, spends his evenings in the
palace heavily guarded by the Praetorians, but sneaks out at night to watch
plays and drink.

| | The theatre plan | The palace plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Area** | Someone with access to the playhouse | Someone with access to the Imperial Palace |
| **Access** | Someone who mixes with actors | Someone in contact with the Praetorian Guard |
| **Means** | Someone who can get poison, for his wine | Someone rich enough to bribe the guard to look away — or do the deed |

## Keeping them happy

- They're **happier when you play their cards** and unhappier the more you
  refuse — so sometimes you play a net-negative card just to keep someone
  sweet.
- **Overusing one person's cards** makes the others suspicious of you.
  *(3.2)*
- **Traits clash.** Military men may not get on with the acting set; the
  socialite and the soldier want opposite things from you.
  *(1. Variables, 3.3)*
- From the earliest notes: each has "different values of like hate" (*my
  reading:* a like↔hate scale towards you), and the tighter your **grasp** on
  them, "the more they either need to be part of it or not".
  *(OneNote › Basics; Chat › Note 1)*

## The conspiracy meeting

In phase 1 there's a **forum — the "shadowy circle"** — where the plot is
discussed. *(OneNote › Basics)* The Conspiracy Phase page describes
meetings where you can sway the pact's decisions, **set up the room** to
influence how you're regarded, and push people in the directions you want.
*(OneNote › Conspiracy Phase)* *My reading* is that these are the same
meeting, but the notes don't say so — see
[Run structure](01-run-structure.md#the-phases).

## Sticky: once in, hard to get out

Co-conspirators should be **very sticky**, for two reasons:

1. **In the fiction**, dropping someone who knows your plans is dangerous —
   they'll want to extract a good price.
2. **In the design**, you should be playing *around* the bad interactions
   between your people, not trimming the deck into a perfect synergy.

So removal happens only through a few events or cards, all at great cost.
Some co-conspirators can remove another — by paying them off (from your
funds), by charming them, and so on.

**Killing one** is always possible but strongly deterred: big suspicion from
the others and worse stats. Realistically **once a run**, when there's no
other way. *(3.4)*

**Conflict —** the earliest note says you "can execute them or leave them
alive" as a normal choice; the later design makes killing a last resort. See
[audit](../audit.md).

## Pivoting

As you learn more about the Emperor, the ideal team changes — but your team
is sticky. Built a deck of soldiers, then discovered he rarely goes on
campaign (or soldiers are too pricey around there)? You may have to pivot to
poisoning at the theatre, with the soldiers and actors at each other's
throats while Pressure climbs. Or try the other way round: **plant ideas in
the Emperor's head** and lead him down a path that suits the team you have.
*(3.3)*

## Betrayal

- Leave someone alive and they may **rat you out** — a chance you're ended.
  *(Basics)*
- High-Pressure blackmailers threaten to **spill the beans** unless you
  appease them. *(1.1 Pressure)*
- **Gullibility** measures how much they think they can lie to you — and a
  socialite who likes you may start doing exactly that. *(1. Variables)*

## Archetypes mentioned so far

Socialite · military man · lout · womaniser · someone who mixes with actors ·
poisoner · rich briber · Praetorian contact.

## From the research

*From the [comparable-games research](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md) (2026-10-12). Suggestions, not
decisions. [Prototype v0.3](../prototype/cards.md) tries the first four.*

- **Baggage that comes from who they are.** Players accept costs they caused
  themselves. *Dominion*'s victory cards clog your deck because you scored;
  *Griftlands*' "Tipsy" cards arrive because you took the drink. A
  co-conspirator's bad card should express who they are (a gossip, a
  brawler, a debtor) and bite where their good card helps.
  *([3.2](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#32-dominion), [3.6](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#36-griftlands))*
- **No bad card is completely unplayable.** *Heat*'s heat cards are the
  price of pushing hard *and* the clog in your hand. Give every bad card a
  use, often trading Pressure for power, so drafting someone for their
  baggage becomes a bet. *([3.3](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#33-heat-pedal-to-the-metal))*
- **Roles at the kill.** *Crusader Kings III*'s 2024 rework cut a scheme's
  agents to about five, each adding speed, secrecy or success. Putting "two
  nemeses in the same plot" is punished, and the base odds are too low to
  scheme alone. Co-conspirators could do the same at the strike.
  *([3.9](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#39-crusader-kings-iii-schemes))*
- **One state per person, printed on the card.** *CK3* dropped random agent
  events as "micro-intensive"; Darkest Dungeon keeps personality to a few
  visible states. Track at most one loyalty state per co-conspirator (say
  Steady, Jumpy or Turned), shown on the card. That also fits
  [D7](../decisions.md). *([3.9](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#39-crusader-kings-iii-schemes),
  [4](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#4-cross-cutting-design-themes))*
- **Avoid two-card combos.** *Griftlands* had to patch combo decks that
  needed "a generator and a finisher" drawn together. Don't make one
  co-conspirator depend on drawing another's card in the same hand.
  *([3.6](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#36-griftlands))*
- **Locked bundles may be new.** The research found no published
  deckbuilder that bundles a person's cards the way [D4](../decisions.md)
  does. That could be a genuine novelty, or a sign it needs a release valve.
  *([6](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#6-implications-for-execute-the-emperor))*
- **Archetypes from history.** The Praetorian officer with a grudge (Cassius
  Chaerea, mocked by Caligula); the chamberlain who sets the scene
  (Parthenius took the blade from the sword under Domitian's pillow); the
  freedman with a bandaged arm (Stephanus hid a dagger in the bandages); the
  mistress with the poison and the athlete who finishes the job (Marcia and
  Narcissus, against Commodus); the secretary who forges a death list
  (Aurelian's). *([5](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#5-setting-research))*
