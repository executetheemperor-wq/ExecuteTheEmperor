# Pressure

> **Decided ([D6](../decisions.md)):** Pressure is the pressure on you from
> your co-conspirators — the push to strike grows, and they get jumpy. It's
> really sticky: unlike every other stat, it comes down in only about **two
> ways**. The notebook's other readings (pressure on the Emperor, the
> pressure to act quickly) are below.

Pressure is the game's **master clock** and its most important global
variable: a 0–100 meta-variable that "defines the course of the game" and is
"an important win or lose condition". It's both **carrot and stick**.
*(OneNote › 1. Variables, 1.1 Pressure)*

## What it stands for

Three things at once: the pressure on **you** to keep the plot moving, the
pressure on **the Emperor** and his position, and the pressure to act
**quickly**. *(OneNote › 1.1 Pressure)*

## Why you want it (the carrot)

- Better co-conspirators become draftable.
- More opportunities to raise your stats.

## Why you fear it (the stick)

- Co-conspirators **force their way in** and blackmail you — wanting money,
  or charm, or forcing weak cards into your hand — "else they'll spill the
  beans".
- Too much pressure spawns **unstable or unreliable** co-conspirators.
  *(OneNote › 3. Co-Conspirators)*

## When it's low

Fewer opportunities. The Emperor's in good standing and co-conspirators cost
more — "Why join in a treacherous plot, when the empire is doing so well?"

## How it goes up

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Time | **+1 every turn** — the clock always ticks. |
| Chance | Some cards and events add pressure randomly. |
| Choice | Powerful cards and co-conspirators add it when used — e.g. a **speech denouncing the Emperor's policy: +5 Pressure**, more influence, more Emperor suspicion. |
| Passive | Some co-conspirators raise the *rate* of gain. |
| Blackmail | Forcing a co-conspirator in costs Pressure. *(3. Co-Conspirators)* |
| Propaganda | A one-time propaganda card raises Pressure. *(3.2 Co-Conspirator Skills)* |

## How it comes down

Hardly ever. Pressure is **"sticky upwards"** — "no more than a handful" of
effects can stall or reduce it. In later drafts, a **new Emperor taking the
throne** knocks it down, but not fully.

## Thresholds

| Pressure | What happens |
|---|---|
| **~80** | Past the point of no return — expect it to snowball to 100 soon whatever you do. |
| **100** | "More or less a lose condition", unless "very certain circumstances" are met. The assassination runs **on autopilot**: someone makes a move, and you just hope the dice land. |

You're meant to start the assassination **well before** 100.

The feel you want at 100: *Five Nights at Freddy's* — the power's gone, the
animatronic's face appears, and you pray it's 6am.

## The feel overall

A **time bomb** in every run that also **snowballs**: cascading consequences
that help you while the real-world tension rises.

You're meant to **manage** it: grow Pressure at a reasonable pace, so its
upsides are live while you keep the drawbacks in check.
*(OneNote › 1.1 Pressure)*

## From the research

*From the [comparable-games research](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md) (2026-10-12). Suggestions, not
decisions. [Prototype v0.3](../prototype/rules.md) tries the first four.*

- **Readable steps, one consequence each.** Klei found that 20 alarm levels
  in *Invisible, Inc.* made it "unclear when the next alarm level would
  actually become a problem". They cut it to 6 levels, each with one
  concrete consequence the player could see coming. For Pressure, that means
  4–5 named bands, with what each one does printed on the track. It would
  fill the "what changes at each band" gap below.
  *([3.7](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#37-invisible-inc))*
- **A threshold fires once.** In *Invisible, Inc.*, bribing a guard lowered
  the alarm, so the next level triggered again when it went back up. Klei
  agreed it was "indeed pretty bad". The Feast mustn't be able to do the
  same. *([3.7](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#37-invisible-inc))*
- **Let the player spend it.** *Blades in the Dark* spends Stress on
  flashbacks; *Heat*'s heat cards are both your fuel and your clog. A
  **Push** (take Pressure to draw cards or refresh the market) turns the
  clock into a temptation, not just a timer.
  *([3.3](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#33-heat-pedal-to-the-metal), [3.13](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#313-blades-in-the-dark))*
- **The clock should pay you.** Ambitious recruits arriving as Pressure
  climbs already do this. In *Clank!*, buying from the market can wake the
  dragon, so greed drives the danger. The rewards on the way should be
  optional but tempting: strong recruits who bring jumpier cards.
  *([3.4](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#34-clank-and-clank-catacombs), [4](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#4-cross-cutting-design-themes))*
- **Breaking should read as character.** Darkest Dungeon's stress works
  because people "break in different ways", not because a number went up.
  Keep jumpy co-conspirators few, visible and dramatic.
  *([3.8](../research/2026-10-12-comparable-games.md#38-darkest-dungeon-and-darkest-dungeon-ii))*

## Gaps

- The "very certain circumstances" that save you at 100.
- What specifically gets better or worse at each band (e.g. 0–30, 30–60,
  60–80).
- How much Pressure drops when a new Emperor takes the throne (the notes
  only say "but not fully").
- Whether hitting 100 forces the assassination to start immediately, and
  with what plan if you haven't made one.
